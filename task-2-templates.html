Writing Task 2 — Templates

Argument skeletons for the eight question types in the forecast. Skeletons, not scripts. The lessons are clear that ideas shape language, not the other way round, so treat these as a frame for your thinking, not a fill-in-the-blank machine.

Universal principles

Before any template, the rules these skeletons rest on. Drawn from the seven lessons.

An argument is a claim plus a reason. Every main idea in every paragraph needs both. A position without a reason is not an argument; a reason that does not logically support the position is not one either.
Your position is shown by your reasoning, not by the phrase "in my view". Sprinkling I believe through the essay does nothing if the logic underneath drifts. The examiner reads the reasoning, not the labels.
Signpost ruthlessly. The examiner is not sitting your reading test. If the link between two ideas is implicit, make it explicit. Firstly, However, As a result are tools, not clichés.
Supporting evidence is reasoning, not invented statistics. "According to a BBC documentary, 85% of people..." tells the examiner you panicked. Use logical, specific, plausible evidence drawn from how the world actually works.
Hedge claims that are not certain. may, could, tend to, in many cases. Predicting that a single policy will solve all the problems is rarely defensible.
Explain perspective when it matters. If your argument depends on conditions in your country, say so. The examiner cannot see Ho Chi Minh City traffic from their desk, and your context is not universal.
Do not jump to a conclusion. A fact that John arrives late does not by itself prove he is uncommitted. Gather the reasoning before you commit to the claim. The same applies to interpreting the question.
Complexity comes from the argument, not from the grammar. A long sentence does not make you sound clever; it makes a confused argument harder to follow. Reach for complex structures when the idea genuinely requires them.

Sanity check before you write

Underline the actual instruction in the prompt. Most band-6.5 scripts answer a question that is adjacent to the one asked but not the one asked. "What are the reasons?" is not "Is it positive or negative?", and "Discuss both views" is not "Argue for the one you like".

1.Agree / Disagree

Question signal

To what extent do you agree or disagree?    Do you agree or disagree?

Recommended approach (from Lesson 7)

Use the balanced approach: acknowledge the opposing view, concede a specific point, then refute it. The one-sided approach is technically allowed but tends to keep candidates at 6.5 through repetition and weakened task response. Pick a position that is not "completely agree" or "completely disagree" unless you genuinely hold it, because total positions are harder to defend.

Introduction (three sentences)

  1. Context. [TOPIC] has become an increasingly debated issue in [CONTEXT: many countries / contemporary society / the workplace, etc.].
  2. Restate the claim in the question. It is sometimes argued that [PARAPHRASED CLAIM FROM PROMPT].
  3. Thesis with position. While I accept that [BRIEF CONCESSION TO OTHER SIDE], I largely [agree / disagree] that [YOUR POSITION], because [REASON 1 IN SUMMARY] and [REASON 2 IN SUMMARY].

Body 1. The opposing view, with concession then refutation

  1. Topic sentence. Some people argue that [OPPOSING POSITION].
  2. Develop their reasoning. Their argument generally rests on the idea that [THEIR REASON]. This is because [EXPLANATION OR CAUSE/EFFECT].
  3. Concede a specific point. Admittedly, [POINT WHERE THEY HAVE A CASE].
  4. Refute (the pivot). However, this view [overlooks / underestimates / does not account for] [LIMITATION].
  5. Explain why your refutation matters. [EXPLANATION, LINKING BACK TO YOUR POSITION].

Body 2. Your main argument

  1. Topic sentence. More importantly, [YOUR POSITION RESTATED AS A CLAIM].
  2. Reason 1 + development. Firstly, [REASON 1]. This means that [EXPLANATION]. As a result, [LOGICAL CONSEQUENCE].
  3. Reason 2 + supporting evidence. Furthermore, [REASON 2]. [DEVELOPMENT or CHAIN OF CAUSE AND EFFECT]. This suggests that [LINK BACK TO POSITION].
  4. Mini-conclusion. Thus, [POSITION REINFORCED IN ONE LINE].

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, although [BRIEF NOD TO OTHER SIDE], I am convinced that [POSITION], because [ONE-LINE SUMMARY OF THE STRONGEST REASON].

Useful connectors for this type

Some people argueAdmittedlyWhileOn the one handHoweverNeverthelessThis view overlooksMore importantlyFurthermoreAs a resultThusIn conclusion
Common pitfalls
  • Presenting the opposing view as if you agree with it (the band-6.5 trap from Lesson 7).
  • Repeating the same idea in different words across both body paragraphs.
  • Stating "in my view X" in the introduction then arguing "Y" in body 1.
  • Inflating the topic with words like catastrophic, disastrous, calamitous when the topic is mundane.
  • Slotting in a final-line "example" that is a clearly invented statistic.

2.Discuss both views and give your opinion

Question signal

Discuss both these views and give your own opinion.

What this question actually demands

Both views must be discussed, with roughly equal development. Your opinion must be stated and must remain clear throughout, not parachuted in at the end. The safest structure is: one body paragraph per view, with your stance woven in. The view you side with should receive slightly fuller development.

Introduction

  1. Context. The question of [TOPIC] divides opinion in [CONTEXT].
  2. State both views. While some hold that [VIEW A], others maintain that [VIEW B].
  3. State your opinion. In my view, [VIEW X] is the more convincing position because [BRIEF REASON].

Body 1. The view you find less convincing

  1. Topic. Those who advocate [VIEW A] tend to argue that [THEIR CORE REASON].
  2. Develop. [EXPANSION]. This is because [EXPLANATION].
  3. Acknowledge merit. There is some force in this argument, particularly [SPECIFIC POINT].
  4. Limit it. Yet this view [overlooks / understates] [LIMITATION], which is why I find it less convincing overall.

Body 2. The view you side with (developed more fully)

  1. Topic. The argument for [VIEW B] is, in my opinion, stronger for several reasons.
  2. Reason 1 + explanation. Firstly, [REASON 1]. [DEVELOPMENT].
  3. Reason 2 + supporting logic. More importantly, [REASON 2]. [LOGICAL EVIDENCE OR CAUSE/EFFECT].
  4. Synthesis. Together, these factors suggest that [POSITION RESTATED].

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, although [VIEW A] has some merit, [VIEW B] is the more compelling position, principally because [STRONGEST REASON].

Useful connectors for this type

While some holdOthers maintainThose who advocateThere is some force inYet this viewOn the other handIn my opinionMore importantlyTogether, these factorsIn conclusion
Common pitfalls
  • Treating the two views unequally. If view A gets a paragraph and view B gets two sentences, task response drops.
  • Failing to state an opinion. The prompt explicitly demands one.
  • Hiding the opinion until the conclusion. It needs to be visible from the introduction.
  • Agreeing with both views. That is fence-sitting, and it confuses the reader about where you actually stand.

3.Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?

Question signal

Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?    Do the advantages of X outweigh its disadvantages?

What this question actually demands

This is an opinion question wearing different clothes. You must take a side. Outweigh is a comparative verb, so you must address both sides and then claim that one is heavier. The side you favour gets the fuller treatment.

Introduction

  1. Context. [TREND OR DEVELOPMENT] has become increasingly common in [CONTEXT].
  2. Acknowledge both sides exist. This brings notable benefits but is not without significant drawbacks.
  3. Position. On balance, I believe the [advantages / disadvantages] clearly outweigh the [other side], because [BRIEF REASON].

Body 1. The weaker side (the one you don't side with)

  1. Topic. Admittedly, there are several [disadvantages / advantages] worth acknowledging.
  2. Point 1 + explanation. Firstly, [POINT]. [EXPLANATION].
  3. Point 2 + explanation. In addition, [POINT]. [EXPLANATION].
  4. Limit / pivot. However, these concerns are [outweighed / mitigated / context-specific] for reasons set out below.

Body 2. The stronger side (your position)

  1. Topic. The [advantages / disadvantages], by contrast, are far more significant.
  2. Reason 1 + cause-effect chain. The most important benefit is that [POINT]. This means [EFFECT 1], which in turn [EFFECT 2].
  3. Reason 2 + supporting evidence. Furthermore, [POINT]. [DEVELOPMENT].
  4. Mini-conclusion. These benefits affect a far broader range of people than the drawbacks identified earlier.

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, while [WEAKER SIDE] cannot be dismissed entirely, the [STRONGER SIDE] clearly outweigh them, because [ONE-LINE SUMMARY].

Useful connectors for this type

AdmittedlyFirstlyIn additionHoweverBy contrastThe most important benefitFurthermoreThis meansWhich in turnOn balanceClearly outweigh
Common pitfalls
  • Listing four advantages and four disadvantages with no claim about which is heavier. That answers a different question.
  • Hedging into invisibility ("there are advantages and disadvantages, so it depends"). Take a side.
  • Choosing the side you can't actually defend because it sounds smarter.

4.What are the advantages and disadvantages?

Question signal

What are the advantages and disadvantages?    Discuss the benefits and the drawbacks of this phenomenon.

What this question actually demands

Descriptive. No opinion is required, although a balanced closing thought is fine. Treat the two sides with equal weight. The skill being tested here is whether you can develop both fairly and connect ideas inside each side cleanly.

Introduction

  1. Context. [TREND OR DEVELOPMENT] has become increasingly visible in [CONTEXT].
  2. Acknowledge complexity. Like many such developments, it carries clear advantages but also notable drawbacks.
  3. Brief preview. Both will be examined in turn.

Body 1. Advantages

  1. Topic. The most evident advantages of [TREND] relate to [BROAD AREA].
  2. Advantage 1 + explanation. Firstly, [POINT]. This is because [REASON], which means [CONSEQUENCE].
  3. Advantage 2 + supporting evidence. A further benefit is that [POINT]. [LOGICAL EVIDENCE].

Body 2. Disadvantages

  1. Topic. On the other hand, there are also significant drawbacks.
  2. Disadvantage 1 + explanation. One concern is that [POINT]. [EXPLANATION OR CAUSE/EFFECT].
  3. Disadvantage 2 + supporting evidence. Another issue is that [POINT]. [DEVELOPMENT].

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, [TREND] offers [BRIEF SUMMARY OF ADVANTAGES] but also brings [BRIEF SUMMARY OF DISADVANTAGES]. Whether the trend is welcome may ultimately depend on [CONTEXT-DEPENDENT FACTOR].

Useful connectors for this type

The most evident advantagesFirstlyThis is becauseA further benefitOn the other handOne concernAnother issueWhether the trend is welcome
Common pitfalls
  • Drifting into opinion when none is asked for. State both sides cleanly.
  • Treating one side as a stub. Equal development matters.
  • Listing without explanation. An "advantage" is only an advantage once you say why it matters.

5.Is this a positive or negative development?

Question signal

Is this a positive or negative development?    Do you think this is a positive or negative development?

What this question actually demands

Functionally identical to agree/disagree. You evaluate a trend and take a position. Treat it like Type 1: acknowledge the side you reject, concede a point, then build the case for your view.

Introduction

  1. Context. [TREND] has become an increasingly defining feature of [CONTEXT].
  2. Acknowledge complexity. Whether this development is welcome remains a matter of debate.
  3. Position. Although it has some [negative / positive] aspects, I regard it as a largely [positive / negative] development, because [REASON 1] and [REASON 2].

Body 1. The opposing evaluation (with concession + refutation)

  1. Topic. Some people argue that this trend is a [negative / positive] development.
  2. Their reasoning. They point out that [OPPOSING REASON]. This is because [EXPLANATION].
  3. Concede. Admittedly, [POINT WORTH ACKNOWLEDGING].
  4. Refute. However, this view [overlooks / overstates] [LIMITATION], which is significant because [EXPLANATION].

Body 2. Your evaluation

  1. Topic. On balance, this development is [positive / negative] because [REASON HEADLINE].
  2. Reason 1 + chain. [POINT] means [EFFECT 1], which in turn [EFFECT 2].
  3. Reason 2 + supporting evidence. Furthermore, [POINT]. [DEVELOPMENT].
  4. Mini-conclusion. The benefits of this trend reach a broader population than its costs.

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, although [BRIEF NOD TO OTHER SIDE], this trend is, in my view, a largely [positive / negative] development, primarily because [SUMMARY].

Useful connectors for this type

It is increasingly common forSome people argueThey point out thatAdmittedlyThis view overlooksOn balanceWhich in turnFurthermorePrimarily because
Common pitfalls
  • Treating it as "advantages and disadvantages" with no evaluative claim. The question asks which, not both.
  • Predicting extreme outcomes ("this will destroy society") without the reasoning to support them.

6.Problem & solution / cause & effect

Question signal

What are the reasons? What can be done?    What problems does this cause? What measures can be taken?    What are the causes? What are the effects?

What this question actually demands

A two-part structure: diagnose, then prescribe. The two halves should be visibly connected. Solutions should target the causes you identified, not float free of them. This is the question type where cause-effect chains earn their keep, so plan with arrows: A → B → C.

Introduction

  1. Context. [ISSUE] has become an increasingly pressing concern in [CONTEXT].
  2. Frame the question. The reasons behind this trend are complex, and any meaningful response must address them at their source.
  3. Brief preview. This essay examines the principal [causes / problems] and proposes [solutions / measures] that target them directly.

Body 1. Causes / problems

  1. Topic. There are several reasons why [ISSUE] has become so widespread.
  2. Cause 1 + chain. The most significant is [CAUSE 1]. This means that [EFFECT 1], which can in turn lead to [FURTHER EFFECT].
  3. Cause 2 + chain. A second factor is [CAUSE 2]. As a result, [EFFECT].
  4. Mini-conclusion. These causes are interconnected, which is why [ISSUE] rarely responds to a single, isolated intervention.

Body 2. Solutions / measures (or effects)

  1. Topic. Addressing this will require [broad approach], with measures aimed at each of the causes above.
  2. Solution 1. Firstly, if [AUTHORITY: governments / schools / employers] were to [ACTION], this could [EXPECTED RESULT]. This would directly address [CAUSE 1].
  3. Solution 2. A further step would be to [ACTION]. By doing so, [BENEFIT], which would help to mitigate [CAUSE 2].
  4. Hedged caveat. These measures are unlikely to eliminate the problem entirely, but they could meaningfully reduce its scale.

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, [ISSUE] stems largely from [CAUSES SUMMARY]. Effective responses must therefore include [SOLUTIONS SUMMARY], applied with care to local conditions.

Useful connectors for this type

The most significant isThis means thatWhich can in turn lead toAs a resultThese causes are interconnectedIf X were toThis couldBy doing soHelp to mitigateUnlikely to eliminate entirely
Common pitfalls
  • Solutions that bear no relation to the causes you just diagnosed. Match them explicitly.
  • Overconfident predictions about solutions: "this will eradicate poverty". Use could, may, would help to.
  • Stacking conditional clauses so long that the reader loses the thread. One cause-effect chain per sentence.
  • Forgetting one of the two parts. If the prompt asks for problems and solutions, you need both.

7.Two-part question

Question signal

Two distinct questions in the prompt, often: What are the reasons? Is this a positive or negative development?    Why? How can we encourage them?

What this question actually demands

Both questions must be answered. Each gets its own body paragraph. The opinion-style second half (positive/negative, advantages/disadvantages, agree/disagree) still requires a clear position. Many candidates lose marks here by answering only the first question well.

Introduction

  1. Context. [PHENOMENON] is increasingly common in [CONTEXT].
  2. Frame both parts. The reasons for this trend are several, and its consequences for individuals and society are mixed.
  3. Thesis answering both briefly. This essay argues that [BRIEF ANSWER TO PART 1], and that the development is, on balance, [BRIEF ANSWER TO PART 2].

Body 1. Part 1 (reasons / causes / why)

  1. Topic. Several factors explain why [PHENOMENON].
  2. Reason 1 + explanation. Firstly, [REASON]. [DEVELOPMENT].
  3. Reason 2 + explanation. A further factor is [REASON]. [DEVELOPMENT].
  4. Mini-conclusion. These factors together help to explain the scale of the trend.

Body 2. Part 2 (evaluation / opinion)

  1. Topic + position. Whether this is a [positive / negative] development is more contested, but I view it as largely [YOUR POSITION].
  2. Reason for position 1. The most important reason is that [REASON 1]. [EXPLANATION].
  3. Reason for position 2 + brief acknowledgement. While [BRIEF CONCESSION], the broader effect of [POINT] is more significant.
  4. Mini-conclusion. On balance, the trend is therefore better understood as [POSITION RESTATED].

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, [PHENOMENON] has arisen primarily because [REASONS IN ONE LINE], and although it brings some drawbacks, it is on balance a [POSITION] development.

Useful connectors for this type

Several factors explainFirstlyA further factorWhether this isThe most important reasonWhileThe broader effectOn balanceHas arisen primarily because
Common pitfalls
  • Answering only part 1 in depth and tacking part 2 onto the conclusion. Both parts need their own paragraph.
  • Missing the opinion in part 2 if it is framed as an evaluation.
  • Forcing part 2 to be the same scale as part 1. The reasons may be many, the evaluation may be simple. That is fine.

8.Direct question

Question signal

A single open question, e.g. Who should fund medical research?    What can arts tell us that science cannot?    How can older people be encouraged to use new technologies?

What this question actually demands

A direct, defended answer. No two-sided structure is required, but the answer must be developed across two body paragraphs with distinct reasons or aspects. Avoid trying to retrofit this into an agree/disagree shape.

Introduction

  1. Context. [TOPIC] raises an important question for [STAKEHOLDERS].
  2. Restate the question. The issue of [PARAPHRASED QUESTION] has no single, obvious answer.
  3. Thesis. In my view, [YOUR ANSWER], primarily because [REASON 1 SUMMARY] and [REASON 2 SUMMARY].

Body 1. First reason or aspect

  1. Topic. The first reason for this position is [REASON 1].
  2. Explanation. [DEVELOPMENT]. This means [CONSEQUENCE].
  3. Supporting evidence. For instance, [SPECIFIC LOGICAL EVIDENCE OR ILLUSTRATION].
  4. Mini-conclusion. Thus, [LINK BACK TO ANSWER].

Body 2. Second reason (typically the stronger one)

  1. Topic. Even more importantly, [REASON 2].
  2. Explanation + chain. [DEVELOPMENT]. As a result, [OUTCOME].
  3. Supporting evidence. [LOGICAL EVIDENCE].
  4. Mini-conclusion. This consideration is decisive because [WHY IT MATTERS].

Conclusion

  1. In conclusion, [YOUR ANSWER RESTATED], because [REASON 1 + REASON 2 IN ONE SENTENCE].

Useful connectors for this type

Has no single answerThe first reasonFor instanceEven more importantlyAs a resultThis consideration is decisiveBecause
Common pitfalls
  • Not actually answering the question. "Who should fund medical research?" needs a who, not a meditation on whether medical research is important.
  • Listing too many shallow reasons. Two reasons developed deeply beat five reasons stated and dropped.
  • Drifting into a different question (one of the band-6.5 errors from Lesson 5).

Hedging language bank

Hedging makes a claim more cautious and therefore more defensible. The lessons emphasise that overclaiming flattens an argument; this bank is the antidote. Use it for any claim that is interpretation, generalisation or prediction.

The rule

Do not hedge definitions or verified facts. Hedge interpretations, generalisations, causal claims and predictions. "If governments were to invest in public transport, this could reduce congestion" is defensible. "This will solve all traffic problems" is not.

A reminder, not a substitute

These templates exist so you can stop spending planning minutes worrying about shape and start spending them on ideas. The shape is the cheapest part of the essay. The thinking is the part the examiner is actually paying for.
