Writing Task 2 — Building an Argument

A concise reference guide to presenting, developing, and strengthening arguments in IELTS Writing Task 2, drawn from Lessons 1–7.

01. What Is an Argument?

An argument is a conclusion supported by one or more reasons (premises). Every time you make a point in your essay, you are making an argument. The key principle is simple: a conclusion must be logically supported by the reasons given for it.

Model Structure

Fruit contains vitamin C, which helps keep us healthy, so the government should encourage everyone to eat more fruit.

Conclusion: The government should encourage everyone to eat more fruit.
Premise 1: fruit contains vitamin C  |  Premise 2: vitamin C keeps us healthy

Key Idea

Writing "In my view" or "I believe" at the start and end of your essay is not enough to make your position clear throughout. Only the language and reasoning in your argument can do that.

02. Clear vs. Unclear Arguments

An argument can fail even when the language is accurate. The reasoning itself may be flawed. There are two common causes.

ProblemExampleWhy it fails
No reason given The government should encourage everyone to eat more fruit. The reader has no basis for accepting this claim.
Reason not logically connected The government should encourage fruit. For example, a study showed that 85% of people eat fruit. The statistic does not explain why the government should act.
Contradictory premises There are many jobs robots will never do, and they will eventually replace humans in most jobs. The two claims directly contradict each other.
Common Pitfall

Extreme vocabulary (catastrophic, disastrous) can undermine an argument if the topic doesn't justify it. Use language whose strength matches your evidence.

03. Argument Type 1: Adding Information

Present several reasons that, taken together, lead to a conclusion. Use this when you have multiple distinct supporting points. Mark emphasis to show which reason carries the most weight.

Connectors

FirstlySecondlyIn additionFurthermoreMoreoverWhat is moreSimilarlyalso

Emphasis (when one reason outweighs others)

More importantlyEven more importantlynot only ... but also
Model Example

There are several reasons why the government collects taxes. Firstly, the money raised can be used to build new roads or public buildings. Secondly, existing infrastructure such as schools and hospitals can be updated or repaired. More importantly, these funds are needed to pay the salaries of government workers, including firefighters and the police. Thus, taxes not only help improve the community but also make it safer.

Skeleton

There are several reasons why [MAIN CLAIM]. Firstly, [REASON 1]. In addition, [REASON 2]. More importantly, [STRONGEST REASON]. Thus, [CONCLUSION].

05. Argument Type 3: Contrast & Negation

Strengthen a point by showing what happens in the opposing situation — "what if we didn't do this?" This is particularly useful in problems/solutions and positive/negative development tasks.

Connectors

If ... does not ...Without ...Unless ...HoweverAlthoughWhile
Model Example

It can put pressure on local charities who have to find food and shelter for the homeless. If the government does not step in to help people who are struggling financially, then this puts even more stress on community programmes, and causes homelessness to rise even further.

Referencing Trap

When linking back to an earlier idea, use a synonym or paraphrase rather than a pronoun. A loose pronoun like "it" or "this" often attaches to the wrong idea, making the conclusion unclear.

❌ In addition, it means that house prices remain very high.
✓ In addition, a lack of accommodation means that house prices remain very high.

06. Explaining & Clarifying Ideas

You may need to explain an idea to define a key term, provide context for your perspective, or rephrase a complex point more simply. Ask yourself: "Will the examiner understand this from their own experience, or does it depend on mine?"

When to clarify perspective

In my country, ...In my city, ...For some people, ...In this context, ...

Rephrasing / clarifying

This means ...which means ...In other words, ...That is to say, ...
Model Example — Showing Perspective

In my country, the vast majority of people drive to school or work. Therefore, if people are spending more time travelling, this means that they are also driving more and more. Because of this, our fossil fuels are likely to be depleted.

Fact vs. Opinion

A fact can be objectively verified. An opinion can only be argued for. Don't present an opinion as though it applies universally. Use modal verbs (can, may) and limiting phrases (some people, in many cases) to hedge claims that aren't universal truths.

Don't Jump to a Conclusion

Reaching a conclusion before you've gathered all the evidence produces an argument the reader cannot follow. If you are interpreting the question through your own experience, make that perspective explicit — don't assume the examiner shares it.

07. Supporting Evidence

Main ideas tend to be general and broad. Supporting evidence is specific and focused. The evidence must logically connect to the main idea — it must show why the idea is reasonable, not simply repeat it.

What good evidence does

  • Explains why a conclusion is valid or reasonable
  • Provides a concrete, specific instance of a broader claim
  • Comes from your own knowledge or experience — not invented statistics

Two ways to order an argument

You can present your conclusion first and then support it, or present evidence first and then conclude. Both are valid; alternating between them adds variety.

Option A — Point then Evidence

Taxes help improve the community while also making it safer. For example, the funds are used to pay the salaries of government workers such as firefighters and the police.

Option B — Evidence then Conclusion

These funds are used to pay the salaries of all government workers, including firefighters and the police. Thus, taxes help improve the community while also making it safer.

Three Things That Kill an Example
  • Invented statistics — they sound implausible and don't prove anything.
  • Repeating the main idea — writing "For example, ..." and then restating the same point is mechanical, not supportive.
  • Trivial evidence for a serious conclusion — the scale of the example must match the scale of the claim.
Key Idea

Finish body paragraphs with a general conclusion, not a specific example. Examples should appear in the middle of the argument, where they support the reasoning — not as the final word.

08. Counterarguments

Acknowledging the opposing view and then showing why you reject it makes your argument stronger than simply ignoring it. The three-step structure below is the clearest way to manage this.

1. Your argument
State your position and the reason for it.
2. Acknowledge counter
Show you understand the opposing view, using hedging language to weaken it.
3. Refute
Explain why you still don't hold the opposing view. Your position must remain clear.

Language to acknowledge (concede)

Admittedly, ...Some people may argue ...Some people might say ...It is true that ...That may be true for some people.

Language to refute

Nevertheless, ...However, ...Although ...While ...Having said that, ...
Model — Complete Agreement with Counterargument

My argument: I completely agree, because staying in the same job is rewarding and increases your chance of promotion.

Counterargument: Some people might say it is boring to stay in the same job.

Concede: It is true that there is less variety.

Refute: However, I believe changing jobs too often can be unsettling, and you may actually learn less.

The Classic Band 6.5 Mistake

Presenting the counterargument without making it clear that you disagree with it makes your position ambiguous. Writing "Governments cannot be held responsible when people lose their home" as a plain statement means the examiner reads it as your position. Always use hedging language to signal distance: "Some people may argue that governments cannot be held responsible..."

09. Hedging Language

Hedging signals how confident you are about a claim. Used carefully, it makes your argument more precise and credible. Used to weaken the counterargument, it strengthens your own position.

Weaken a claim (less certain)

couldmightmaypossiblysomesometimesit is unlikely thata few

Strengthen a claim (more certain)

willwouldmustclearlydefinitelygenerallyis likely tooftenin most cases
Hedging in Action — Weakening a Counterargument

Staying in the same job may have some benefits, but I still think it is better to have more variety.

The underlined hedging (may have some) casts doubt on the counterargument without dismissing it outright — which makes the refusal to accept it more convincing, not less.

Quick Checklist
  • Does each main point have a logical reason behind it?
  • Are your conclusions supported by specific evidence, not invented statistics?
  • Is your position clear throughout — especially when discussing the opposing view?
  • Have you used referencing (synonyms/paraphrase) to avoid ambiguous pronouns?
  • Does the strength of your language match your certainty about each claim?
