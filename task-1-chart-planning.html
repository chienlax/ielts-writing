The Planning Scratchpad:
A Chart-by-Chart Guide

Three minutes of structured note-taking before any sentence is typed. The framework is the same for every chart, but the middle step adapts to what each chart is actually holding. This guide covers all five chart types you will meet in the academic test: line, pie, bar, table, and mixed.

Computer-Based Test Planning Phase Only Charts & Tables ~3 Minutes Total 5 Chart Types

01. The Three-Step Framework

Every chart task is planned the same way: a details strip, a key-features table, and a paragraph plan. What changes between chart types is the shape of Step 2 and the grouping logic of Step 3. Step 1 barely changes; the symbol set is identical across all five types.

Step 1 Details Strip 30 seconds
Step 2 Key Features Table 90-120 seconds
Step 3 Paragraph Plan 30 seconds

Step 1 - Details Strip

A single line across the top of the scratch surface that captures the chart's metadata. The contents feed directly into the introduction sentence; once written, the screen does not need to be consulted again for that information.

Step 1 template
Chart: [type]  |  Unit: [Y-axis label]  |  Where: [country / context]  |  When: [time frame]  |  N: [number of categories]

Whether the data is historical, projected, or both, belongs here. So does any unusual scale feature, such as a Y-axis that does not start at zero.

Step 2 - Key Features Table

This is the only step where structure changes. The table always has four columns; the middle two columns change depending on what the chart holds. Five variants are detailed below in Section 3. The right-most column is always reserved for flags.

Step 3 - Paragraph Plan

Two short lines beneath the table: P1: [categories] and P2: [categories]. The logic behind these groupings depends on the chart type. The most common patterns are grouping by trend (line charts), by chart (multi-pie comparisons), by performance tier (tables), and by growth pattern (bar charts).

The Promise Rule

Every flag written in Step 2 should reach the overview or a body paragraph. Anything that does not appear in the writing was either not a key feature, or has been forgotten. Either is recoverable at the planning stage but costly afterwards.

02. The Symbol Toolkit (Universal)

The same shorthand is used across all chart types. Speed depends on consistency, so settle on these symbols early and stop inventing new ones in the test.

★
Highest or lowest overall
↑↑↑
Dramatic or notable increase
↑↑
Strong but not exceptional rise
↓↓↓
Dramatic or notable decrease
!
Anomalous or unexpected
≈
Roughly stable or near-equal
→
Convergence; two values meet or cross
}
Grouping bracket; ties categories together

What each symbol licenses you to say in the writing:

  • ★ licenses superlative language: "the highest figures throughout", "by far the lowest".
  • ↑↑↑ / ↓↓↓ license evaluative comparatives: "the most dramatic growth", "the sharpest decline".
  • ! licenses contrast phrases: "the only category to...", "notably, however...".
  • ≈ licenses stability language: "remained roughly stable", "near parity".
  • → licenses crossover language: "by 1998, A had overtaken B", "nearly matching".

03. Adapting the Scratchpad to Each Chart Type

The next five sections walk through one chart type each. The structure is identical: properties first, scratchpad adaptation second, grouping logic third, and a fully worked example from the project last.

1
Line Chart
Time series, continuous lines, start-to-end change

Distinguishing properties

Structure Categories are visualised as continuous lines on a shared time axis. Every line has a starting value and an ending value, with intermediate movement that may rise, fall, peak, dip, or cross other lines.
What the chart is asking for A comparison of how each category changed over the period, and how the categories compare to one another. The reader expects evaluative language about direction, magnitude, and timing.
Common traps Too many data points encourage transcription. Tense slips appear when historical data and projected data sit on the same chart. Crossovers and short-lived dips are easy to miss when scanning lines individually.

Step 2 adaptation

The default four-column table fits line charts directly. Approximate start value, approximate end value, and a flag are sufficient for every category. Where the chart has more than two endpoints worth noting (such as an interior peak), add the year in parentheses next to the flag.

CategoryStart valueEnd valueFlag
[name]~[value]~[value]★ / ↑↑↑ / ↓ / ! / ≈ / →

Step 3 grouping logic

Group categories by trend type. The most natural pairings are:

  • Steady risers vs. dramatic risers: categories that grew gently against those that grew sharply.
  • Risers vs. fallers: when the chart contains movement in both directions.
  • The pack vs. the anomaly: when most categories behave similarly but one category does something different.

Worked example

Task

The graph below shows the percentage of households with different kinds of technology in the U.S. from 1995 to 1999. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Step 1 - Details strip
Chart: Line graph  |  Unit: % of households (0-90)  |  Where: U.S.  |  When: 1995-1999 (yearly)  |  N: 5
Category19951999Flag
Washing machine~60%~80%★ highest throughout
Computer~40%~75%↑↑ steady; → near-ties phone by '99
Phone (mobile)~18%~75%↑↑↑ biggest single jump
Internet~10%~50%★ lowest start; ↑↑
DVD player~25%~40%! dipped to ~15% by '96, then ↑
Step 3 - Paragraph plan
P1 (steady, higher start): Washing machine + Computer
P2 (lower start, dramatic / anomalous): Phone + Internet + DVD player
Notes on this example

The DVD player exclamation is what separates a Band 6 answer from a Band 7 one; without flagging the dip, the writer will produce a generic "all rose" overview and miss the only category that moved against the trend. The convergence between computer and phone in 1999 is worth marking with →, since it gives the body paragraph a natural comparative sentence.

2
Pie Chart
Proportional segments, no inherent time axis

Distinguishing properties

Structure Each pie shows a 100% total broken down into proportional segments. A single pie is a snapshot; multiple pies appear when the test compares the same categories across time, or compares different breakdowns of related data.
What the chart is asking for A reading of which slices dominate, which are minor, and (in multi-pie tasks) how the proportions shift. Evaluative language about size and rank matters more than directional language about change.
Common traps Mixing fractions with percentages confuses the reader. Pie charts with many small slices invite over-transcription. The "Other" category is often dismissed too quickly; include it when it exceeds 5%, or when it grows or shrinks meaningfully across pies.

Step 2 adaptation

Replace the "Start / End" columns with one column per pie. The flag column shifts toward proportional rank rather than directional change.

CategoryPie 1 (%)Pie 2 (%)Flag
[name][value][value]★ largest / ★ smallest / ≈ near-equal / ! unexpected shift

For a single pie, drop the second pie column entirely. For three or more pies (as in the tea production / consumption / profits task), use as many columns as there are pies and adjust the flag to capture which pie a category dominates in.

Step 3 grouping logic

Three options, ranked by frequency:

  1. By pie (most common for multi-pie tasks). P1 = first pie's main slices, P2 = second pie's main slices. The overview compares the two.
  2. By dominance tier (works for any pie). P1 = dominant slices, P2 = minor slices.
  3. By behaviour across time (works for comparative pies showing the same categories over two periods). P1 = categories that grew, P2 = categories that shrank.

Worked example

Task

The pie charts below show the percentage of five types of food sold by a supermarket in 2003 and 2013. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Step 1 - Details strip
Chart: Two pie charts  |  Unit: % of food sales  |  Where: one supermarket  |  When: 2003 vs 2013  |  N: 5 categories per pie
Category2003 (%)2013 (%)Flag
Vegetables2523★ largest in 2003; ≈ slight ↓
Meat2125★ largest in 2013; ↑↑
Seafood1921↑ moderate rise
Eggs2014↓↓↓ biggest fall
Fruits1517★ smallest both years; slight ↑
Step 3 - Paragraph plan
P1 (categories that rose): Meat + Seafood + Fruits
P2 (categories that fell or held): Vegetables + Eggs
Alternative grouping: P1 = top three by share in 2013; P2 = bottom two. Either organisation works, provided it is signalled clearly at the start of each paragraph.
Notes on this example

The key reversal in this task is meat overtaking vegetables as the largest category. That single observation belongs in the overview. The Eggs fall is the headline figure for body paragraph two; without it, the answer flattens into "everything roughly the same".

Three-pie variant

For tasks with three pies showing different breakdowns of related data (such as tea production by country, tea consumption by region, and how profits are distributed), use one column per pie. Flag where each category appears uniquely or dominates. The grouping logic shifts to by pie: one body paragraph per chart, with the overview weaving the three together.

3
Bar Chart
Discrete bars; grouped, clustered, or stacked

Distinguishing properties

Structure Categories are visualised as discrete bars of differing heights. Three common configurations: a single bar per category (one snapshot), grouped bars (same categories across multiple time points or groups), and stacked bars (each bar broken into proportional segments, behaving like a flattened pie).
What the chart is asking for For single-snapshot bar charts, a reading of rank and proportion. For multi-time-point or grouped bars, a reading of change, similar to a line chart, with the added option of comparing within a time point as well as across time.
Common traps Treating a multi-time bar chart as if it were a sequence of separate pie charts loses the time dimension. Treating it as if it were a line chart can lose the discrete-category framing. The structure of the answer should reflect what the chart is doing.

Step 2 adaptation

Replace the "Start / End" columns with one column per time point (or per group, if the bars are clustered by group rather than time). Two columns for two-time-point bar charts, three for three-time-point, and so on.

CategoryTime 1Time 2(Time 3...)Flag
[name][value][value][value]★ / ↑↑↑ / ↓↓↓ / ! / ≈

For stacked bar charts, treat each segment within a stack as its own category. The columns then become "Stack 1 share / Stack 2 share / ...". Flags emphasise which segment dominates each stack and how the dominance shifts.

Step 3 grouping logic

Two main options:

  • By behaviour across time: categories that rose together, categories that fell, and categories that went against the trend.
  • By identity: when the categories naturally divide into types (for example, imports vs exports, or genders, or age bands), let the natural division drive the paragraphs.

Worked example

Task

The bar chart below shows the number of tourists in three nations who travelled to New Zealand in 1999 and 2009. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Step 1 - Details strip
Chart: Grouped bar chart  |  Unit: number of tourists  |  Where: New Zealand  |  When: 1999 vs 2009  |  N: 3 source nations
Category19992009Flag
UK~1.0M~1.6M★ highest in 2009; ↑↑
US~0.8M~1.5M↑↑↑ near-doubled
Japan~1.5M~1.14M! only fall; was ★ highest in 1999
Step 3 - Paragraph plan
P1 (rose): UK + US
P2 (fell, against trend): Japan
Notes on this example

Two flags carry this answer: the Japan reversal (was highest in 1999, now no longer the leader), and the US near-doubling. Without these, the overview slumps into a generic statement that "more tourists came overall", which the chart does not even fully support given Japan's fall.

Three-time-point variant

For tasks such as recycled waste by material across 2000, 2005, and 2010, add a third value column. Flag the magnitude of change across the full window, and note any non-monotonic movement (a dip followed by recovery, or a peak followed by decline). The paragraph plan typically groups by total growth rate: biggest growers in P1, moderate or anomalous categories in P2.

Stacked bar variant

Treat each colour band as its own category. Flags emphasise which segment dominates each stack, and how the relative proportions shift between stacks. The paragraph plan typically follows the natural identity division: in a transport task showing imports vs exports across years, P1 = imports, P2 = exports, with the overview comparing totals across the two stacks.

4
Table
Rows and columns, no visual trend, two-pass scan

Distinguishing properties

Structure Rows are categories, columns are metrics or time points. There is no visual line to follow, so the eye has to do the comparison work that a chart would otherwise do for you.
What the chart is asking for A reading of which rows dominate, which underperform, and where the spread is largest. If the columns are time points, directional change matters too. If the columns are different metrics, rank and relative position matter more.
Common traps Reading row-by-row only misses column-wide patterns (such as one country dominating every metric). Reading column-by-column only misses row-wide spread (such as one row having a much wider range than the others). Both passes are needed.

Step 2 adaptation

The scratchpad uses one column per data column in the source table, plus a flag column. For larger tables (six or more data columns), keep only the columns you intend to discuss; the scratchpad is a planning tool, not a transcription.

RowCol 1Col 2Col 3 ...Flag
[name][value][value][value]★ / ! / ≈ / spread note

The two-pass scan is the difference between a Band 6 and a Band 7 table answer:

  1. Pass 1 (column-wise): scan each column for its highest and lowest value. Mark a (1) next to the highest and (3) next to the lowest in each column. Patterns emerge when one row repeatedly picks up the (1) marker.
  2. Pass 2 (row-wise): for each row, look at the spread between its highest and lowest value. A wide spread is worth flagging; a flat row is also worth flagging.

Step 3 grouping logic

Two natural options:

  • By performance tier: high performers in P1, low performers in P2.
  • By behaviour across columns (when columns are time points): rising rows in P1, falling or volatile rows in P2.

Worked example

Task

The table shows the changes in carbon production in four countries from 1995 to 1999. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Step 1 - Details strip
Chart: Table  |  Unit: kg per day  |  Where: 4 African countries  |  When: 1995-1999 (yearly)  |  N: 4 rows × 5 years
Country199519971999Flag
Nigeria2.0M1.9M2.3M★ highest every year; ≈ stable around 2M
Algeria040K50K! started from zero; ↑↑ from 1997
Congo270K230K100K↓↓↓ only consistent faller
Botswana210K15K75K! volatile; crashed then partial recovery
Step 3 - Paragraph plan
P1 (dominant or rising): Nigeria + Algeria
P2 (falling or volatile): Congo + Botswana
Notes on this example

Three features carry the overview: Nigeria's dominance (column-wise scan), Congo's consistent decline (row-wise direction), and Algeria's emergence from zero (an anomaly that would be missed without the row-wise pass). Botswana's volatility is the kind of detail that earns a Band 7 lexical range marker if reported with precision ("crashed then partially recovered") rather than transcribed value-by-value.

5
Mixed Charts
Two visuals; two mini-scratchpads plus a synthesis note

Distinguishing properties

Structure Two distinct visuals are presented together. Common pairings include table + line graph, bar chart + pie chart, and pie chart + table. The two visuals usually show related but different aspects of the same topic.
What the chart is asking for A reading of each visual on its own terms, plus a synthesis that captures any connection, contrast, or implication between them. The overview is where the synthesis lives; the body paragraphs typically take one visual each.
Common traps Treating the two visuals as completely separate tasks loses the synthesis marks. Treating them as one combined visual leads to confused paragraphing. The clearest organisation is one body paragraph per visual, with the overview drawing the connection.

Step 2 adaptation

Run the appropriate scratchpad format for each visual separately. If one is a line graph, use the start-end-flag structure for it. If the other is a table, use the row-by-column scan for it. Below the two mini-scratchpads, add a synthesis note: two or three bullet points that capture what the two visuals say together.

Mini-scratchpad A (chart 1)
[run appropriate format].........
Mini-scratchpad B (chart 2)
[run appropriate format].........
Synthesis note (feeds the overview)
• [observation tying the two visuals together]
• [a contrast or unexpected link]
• [an umbrella claim that covers both]

Step 3 grouping logic

The default is one paragraph per visual: P1 covers the first chart, P2 covers the second. The overview weaves both together using the synthesis note.

For tasks where the two visuals share a structural division (such as imports vs exports presented across two charts), the alternative is to group by that shared division across both visuals. This is harder to write and only worth attempting when the structural division is much more salient than the chart-by-chart split.

Worked example

Task

The bar chart below shows the number of tourists in three nations who travelled to New Zealand in 1999 and 2009. The graph below shows the number of tourists who spent their holiday in two different ways. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Step 1 - Details strip
Chart: Bar chart + line graph  |  Unit: number of tourists  |  Where: New Zealand  |  When: 1999, 2004, 2009  |  N: 3 source nations (bar) + 2 holiday types (line)
Mini-scratchpad A - Bar chart (tourists by nation)
Nation19992009Flag
UK~1.0M~1.6M★ highest in 2009
US~0.8M~1.5M↑↑↑ near-doubled
Japan~1.5M~1.14M! only fall
Mini-scratchpad B - Line graph (holiday type)
Type19992004 (peak)2009
Staying at resorts~1.65M~2.37M ★ peak~1.05M ↓↓↓ from peak
Backpacking~0.25M~0.75M~1.05M ↑↑↑ steady rise
→ The two converged in 2009 at ~1.05M.
Synthesis note
• Total tourist numbers shifted in composition (Japan fell while UK/US rose).
• Holiday style also shifted: resort-stays peaked then collapsed; backpacking grew steadily.
• By 2009, the two holiday types met, suggesting a structural change in how tourists chose to travel.
Step 3 - Paragraph plan
P1: Bar chart - tourists by nation (3 source countries, contrasting movements).
P2: Line graph - holiday types (resort decline, backpacking growth, convergence in 2009).
Notes on this example

The synthesis note is the only step that does not exist in single-chart tasks. Without it, the overview becomes two separate one-sentence statements ("more tourists came; resort stays declined") rather than a coherent reading of what the data shows together. The convergence in 2009 is the key crossover, marked with →, and earns its place in the overview.

05. Common Pitfalls at the Planning Stage

Pitfall 1
Skipping the scratchpad to "save time". The three minutes spent here are recouped many times over during writing, because the decisions have already been made. Going straight to typing produces an answer that lists data rather than analyses it, which is the Band 5 to Band 6 trap.
Pitfall 2
Transcribing rather than selecting. The scratchpad is a planning tool. Approximate figures and a flag per category are enough; precise values can be read off the screen at the moment they appear in a sentence. Filling the scratchpad with exact numbers wastes the time the scratchpad was meant to save.
Pitfall 3
Flagging too much, or too little. A useful scratchpad has between one and three flags per category. Zero flags means the category will produce a flat data sentence; four or more flags means the category will dominate a paragraph it should not.
Pitfall 4
Groupings that exist in the head but not on the page. The Step 3 paragraph plan should be visible and concrete. "P1: categories that grew, P2: categories that fell" is concrete; "P1: the interesting ones, P2: the others" is not. If the grouping cannot be named cleanly in three words, it has not been chosen yet.
Pitfall 5
Altering the data. Adding percentages together, converting awkward percentages into "approximately a third", or splitting a column to invent sub-totals all create extra checking work for the examiner. The scratchpad should hold the data as the chart presents it.
Pitfall 6
(Computer test only) Returning to the screen too often. The scratchpad's purpose is to capture everything the writing needs, so that the screen becomes a reference rather than the source of every sentence. A well-built scratchpad is consulted constantly while writing; the chart on screen, only occasionally.
Pitfall 7
Forgetting the synthesis note in mixed-chart tasks. Without it, the answer becomes two separate single-chart answers stitched together, with no overview that actually overviews. The synthesis note is short (two or three lines) but it is what raises a mixed-task overview from Band 6 to Band 7+.

06. The Whole Process in One Page

From planning to writing

By the time the scratchpad is complete, the introduction is half-written (Step 1 contains every component of it), the overview is half-written (the flags from Step 2 are its ingredients), and the body structure is fully decided (Step 3 names the two paragraphs and which categories go in each). The writing stage is then a matter of converting structured planning into prose, rather than thinking and writing at the same time.

A note on chart variants the test might throw

Variants beyond the five core types (stacked column charts, horizontal bar charts, area charts) all reduce to one of the cases above. A horizontal bar chart is structurally a bar chart with the axes rotated; the scratchpad is identical. An area chart is structurally a line chart with the area below each line filled; the scratchpad is identical. When something unfamiliar appears, ask which of the five it most resembles, and use that scratchpad.
