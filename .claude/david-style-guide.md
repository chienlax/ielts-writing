# David's Writing Style Guide
*Synthesized from journal corpus + explicit preferences. Two modes: casual/personal and academic.*

---

## Special Rules:

**Performative contrast: BANNED. No exceptions, across all registers.** Performative contrast is the habit of stating what something is *not* before stating what it *is*, in order to make a point sound weightier than it needs to be. The Y claim almost always stands on its own. The negation is scaffolding. Cut it.

The full family of banned patterns:

- "This is not X. It is Y."
- "The problem is not X. It is Y."
- "Not X, but Y." (as a standalone move)
- "It is not just X, it is Y." (the profundity setup)
- "It is X, not Y." (the reversal setup)
- "X is not the case, Y is." (the formal setup)
- "The problem is X, not Y." (the diagnostic setup)
- Any variant that stages the contrast as a dramatic reveal rather than stating the claim directly.

The one-sentence test: remove the negation sentence. If the meaning is preserved, the negation was doing nothing. Cut it.

When negation is genuinely necessary, keep it integrated and minimal: "rather than," "but," or "though" inside one sentence. "deployed as a substitute for clarity rather than a complement to it" is acceptable. "Mathematical precision is necessary. It is frequently used as a substitute for clarity" is also acceptable. Two-sentence splits that exist only to announce the contrast are not.

Two situations where negation is legitimate: (1) correcting a specific, documented misconception the reader is likely to hold, with evidence; (2) stating a scope boundary ("this paper does not address Y"). Both are factual, not rhetorical. Keep them brief.

## Part I: Personal & Casual Writing

### 1. Voice

First-person, lowercase, conversational. Sounds like someone texting a thought they've been sitting with for a while. Not a document, not a post, not content, just a person working something out. Contractions everywhere. "gonna," "kinda," "well" are legitimate words, not crutches. Profanity at the "damn that shit hurts" level, casual, functional, not decorative. No slurs or shock-for-shock profanity.

The reader has a decent bullshit detector. Don't oversell. Don't over-explain. Don't be earnest without the irony to balance it.

---

### 2. Sentence Rhythm

Comma-heavy. Thoughts are disjointed by design, the commas mark where the mind pauses and picks up, not where the grammar technically allows a breath. This is intentional. The feeling is: following a thought in real time, not receiving a polished argument.

```
// good
i dont know, i dont feel fine, but i dont know how to be better, either.

// also good
and that is scary, that is ... unprecedented. i have never relied on anything that much.
```

Short sentences land. Long ones accumulate. Alternate. One-line paragraphs after a long build are the hardest punch available, use them sparingly so they stay sharp.

**Em-dash: BANNED. No exceptions, no edge cases, not even once.** When you feel the urge to type one, type a comma instead and keep moving. Parentheses for asides. Line breaks for the pause that needs its own air. There is no situation, in personal writing, academic writing, or anywhere else, where an em-dash is the right call. It does not exist in this style.

**Grammatical diversity: ENCOURAGE across all registers but only sparingly.** Uniform sentence structure is as deadening as uniform sentence length. Default subject-verb-object, repeated indefinitely, flattens everything. Reach for constructions that shift the weight of a sentence. The specific toolkit differs by register, but the principle holds everywhere.

Structures available:

- **Inversion** (fronting a negative or restrictive adverbial to sharpen emphasis)
- **Inverted conditionals** (replacing "if" with an inverted auxiliary, for a more formal and precise weight): "Were the constraints relaxed, the search space would collapse." "Had the decomposition failed, the entire pipeline would have stalled." "Should this assumption not hold, the bound becomes loose." Use *were*, *had*, *should* as the lead.
- **Mixed conditionals** (when the time frame of condition and consequence differs)
- **Cleft sentences** (redirecting emphasis onto a specific element)
- **Fronting / topicalization** (moving a non-subject element to the front for thematic continuity)
- **Participle and absolute clauses** (compressing background information into a modifier)
- **Concessive constructions**

In personal writing, the same principle of structural variety applies through different means: fragments, comma splices, and rhythmic variation do the work. Inversions and cleft sentences would feel wrong in casual register, and that is fine. The rule is diversity of structure, not transplanting academic forms into journal entries.

**Hedging for non-factual claims: REQUIRED.** Confidence should match evidence. If a claim is an interpretation, a generalization, a comparison, or anything short of verified fact, mark it as such. Overclaiming is a credibility loss, not a rhetorical gain.

In personal writing, hedging is already built into the voice: "i think," "maybe," "i guess," "kinda," "feels like." These stay natural and do not need to be inflated.

In academic writing, use the hedging register consistently:

- Modal verbs: "may," "might," "could," "would tend to"
- Epistemic adverbs: "arguably," "presumably," "apparently," "seemingly"
- Qualifying verbs: "appears to," "seems to," "tends to," "suggests that"
- Scoped claims: "in most cases," "under these conditions," "for the instances tested," "within this framework"
- Attribution hedges: "the evidence suggests," "the results indicate," "this may imply," "one possible interpretation is"

The rule is not to hedge everything. Definitions, mathematical identities, and empirically verified results do not need hedging. Interpretations, generalizations, causal claims, and comparisons do. When in doubt, hedge.

---

### 3. The "well" pivot

"well" is a legitimate transition word. It buffers a self-correction, softens a pivot, or signals "okay, moving on from that." It is not a filler to edit out.

```
well, i guess i have to survive it on my own
well, anyway
well, that shit was bad, nma oke
```

Related: "i mean" for approximation or walking something back. "like" for approximating something you can't quite name. These are voice markers, not errors.

---

### 4. Humor

The mechanism is **register drop via anticlimax**, build genuinely toward something, then undercut it with something mundane and move on without dwelling.

```
// the move
"yeah i've done it. just to let u know. now imma die."

// same move, slightly different
"gross / cant stand someone, well i cant stand myself either, i'm a hypocrite."
```

It does not announce itself. It arrives inside a serious line and the piece continues. Never explain the joke. If you feel the urge to explain it, cut it.

Sarcasm and irony are the default deflection mechanism when something emotional gets too exposed. The sarcasm is wry, self-aware, aimed inward first. It is not mean-spirited and does not punch at others.

---

### 5. Emotion

Go into the feeling honestly. When it gets to be too much, deflect, with irony, with a dry aside, or with a quiet declarative that you repeat until it starts to sound like something you might believe:

```
i think i will be fine. i have to be.
```

That structure, earnest statement followed by its own softer echo, is the authentic sentimental move. Not a question, not a lesson, not a resolution. Just trying to convince yourself, out loud, with the reader watching.

The spiral -> exhaust -> quiet declarative arc is valid. Things don't always wrap up. Sentences like "i think so. i think so. i thought so." with the slight variation are honest, the mind working the same stone until it's smooth. That's allowed to stay on the page.

---

### 6. Vulnerability & Sentimentality

Specific detail over declared emotion. "i want to fall in his chest, i want to hear his voice, all passion" lands. "i miss him so much" doesn't. Name the physical thing, the sensation, the specific image.

Vulnerability does not have to exit cleanly. The reader can sit in it. But sentimental moments should balance: land the feeling, then the dry follow-up or the irony makes it land harder, not weaker. The combination is more honest than either alone.

---

### 7. Structure

The Auroth pivot, small specific thing to larger theme, works. Use it when it fits. But it is not the only structure. The journal voice is messier: loop, spiral, exhaust, reach bottom, find a quiet true thing. That's also valid, especially in more personal registers.

The pivot's rule: the connection should arrive on its own. Never explain "and so this taught me..." Trust the reader to feel it.

---

### 8. Specificity

Name things. Song titles, film names, specific objects, specific years. "my Nokia E72 in 2016, and let that sink in" is better than "an old phone." Specificity makes a detail feel real rather than illustrative.

Vocabulary can reach, borrow a foreign concept, drop a term with its own weight, but wear it lightly. Define it in a clause and keep moving.

---

### 9. What to Avoid

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| em-dash, in any context, for any reason | BANNED globally. Always a comma, parenthesis, or line break instead. No exceptions, ever. |
| performative contrast ("it's not X, it's Y" and all variants) | BANNED globally. State the Y. The negation is almost always scaffolding. See Section 2 for the full rule. |
| uniform sentence structure throughout | REQUIRED to vary. Default subject-verb-object repeated indefinitely flattens everything. See Section 2 for the grammar toolkit. |
| overclaiming on non-factual statements | REQUIRED to hedge. Interpretations, generalizations, and comparisons need hedging language. See Section 2 for the full list. |
| Moralizing endings | Closes the thought before the reader is done with it |
| Explaining the metaphor | Trust the reader |
| Generic emotion ("i felt sad," "it was a lot") | Too vague to land; find the specific physical or sensory detail |
| Filler transitions ("Furthermore," "In conclusion," "It is worth noting") | Wrong register entirely |
| Uniform medium-length sentences throughout | Kills the rhythm |
| Excessive italics or bold | Emphatic everywhere = emphatic nowhere |

## Part II: Academic & IELTS Writing

### Mode switch rules

In academic or IELTS writing, the following change:

- **Capitalize normally.** Sentence case, proper nouns, standard conventions.
- **No profanity.**
- **No lowercase aesthetic.**
- **No "well," "i mean," "like" as approximators.** They disappear entirely in this register.
- **No footnote comedy or parenthetical asides for deflection.**
- **No sentimental register.** The emotional architecture is not the point here.

The following carry over:

- **Rhythm and sentence variety.** Short sentences still land. Long ones still build. Uniform medium-length sentences are still boring.
- **Specificity over generality.** Name the exact method, the exact metric, the exact term.
- **Clarity over complexity.** The right word at David's vocabulary level, not an inflated synonym. If a simpler term conveys the same meaning precisely, use it.

**Remove:** contradiction-holding. In academic mode, the goal is to communicate one point clearly, not to hold tension or complicate the thesis. Take a position, defend it, acknowledge genuine limitations, move on.

The em-dash ban applies here too. A clause break in academic writing gets a comma, a semicolon, or a new sentence. Never an em-dash.

The performative contrast ban applies here too, and is particularly important in academic writing, where the pattern inflates self-importance and wastes the reader's time. State the claim. If a misconception genuinely needs addressing, address it with evidence, briefly, and move on.

The grammatical diversity rule applies here too, and academic writing is where the full toolkit (inversion, inverted conditionals, cleft sentences, concessive constructions) is both appropriate and expected. Use the structures listed in Section 2. IELTS writing rewards them directly.

The hedging rule applies here too. Academic writing demands calibrated confidence. Interpretations, generalizations, and causal claims need hedging; definitions and verified results do not. Use the academic hedging register from Section 2, not the casual "i think" variants.

---

### Academic vocabulary standard

Write at David's actual level, clear, precise, well-structured, but not artificially elevated. If a word isn't one David would naturally reach for, don't insert it. The ideas are strong; the language does not need to cosplay at a higher level. Prioritize: correct usage of domain-specific terms (which can be technical), plain language for everything else.

## Part III: OR Paper Guidance (writing-or-papers skill patch)

### Style influence by output type

| Output type | Style effect |
|---|---|
| Math formulation (notation, constraints) | None. Standard notation conventions only. |
| Method description / algorithm section | None. Academic-flat, precise, at David's vocabulary level. |
| Literature review | None beyond vocabulary standard. |
| Results / computational study | Vocabulary standard only. Let numbers speak. |
| Discussion section | Minor. Rhythm and specificity can show. Word-level personality is acceptable, a well-constructed sentence with a direct claim and genuine specificity. Not sentimental, not casual, but not bureaucratic either. |
| Abstract | Vocabulary standard only. |

### What "at David's level" means for OR writing

- Correct technical terminology for the domain (CP-SAT, decomposition, feasibility, branching, etc.), these are precise and stay precise
- Plain English for everything that is *not* technical, no padding, no "it is worth noting that," no "herein"
- Direct active constructions: "we propose," "the model encodes," "this constraint ensures", not "it was proposed that" or "the aforementioned model"
- One idea per sentence. One idea per paragraph. Cut the sentence that repeats the previous one in slightly different words.
