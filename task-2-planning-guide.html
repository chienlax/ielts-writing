Writing Task 2 — Essay Planning

A step-by-step guide to the T.P.W.C approach — the thinking and planning framework that turns a blank page into a clear, organised argument.

01. The T.P.W.C Framework

The whole 40-minute writing process breaks down into four stages. The most important insight is that the first ten minutes of thinking and planning are the writing — skipping them guarantees a weaker essay, not a longer one.

Stage What you do Time
Think Read the question carefully. Identify the topic, focus, issue, and relevant themes. Decide on your initial position. 1–2 min
Plan Brainstorm ideas under your themes. Think critically — connect ideas, delete irrelevant ones, reach conclusions. Draft first and last sentences for each paragraph. 7–8 min
Planning total 10 min
Write Write the full essay following your plan. Do not invent ideas on the fly — your plan is the map. 18–20 min
Check Review argument clarity and position; fix language and repetition; proofread for slips. 8–10 min
Writing + checking total 30 min
Key Idea

Planning is writing. Cutting your planning time to write more does not produce a better essay — it produces a longer, weaker one.

02. Step 1 — Think

Before writing a single word of your essay, spend one to two minutes dissecting the question. Misreading it at this stage compounds into every paragraph that follows.

What to identify in the question

Work through these four elements in order:

  • General topic — the broad subject area (e.g. technology, education, the environment)
  • Focus — who or what is specifically affected
  • Issue — the precise point you are being asked to discuss
  • Themes — related concepts that will naturally come up when writing about this topic

Identifying themes early is particularly useful: they become the headings on your plan, guide your brainstorming, and reduce the risk of introducing irrelevant ideas later.

Worked Example

Question: "Many people believe that working from home is more beneficial than working in an office. To what extent do you agree?"

  • General topic: work and employment
  • Focus: employees / workers in general
  • Issue: whether remote work is more beneficial than office work
  • Themes: productivity, wellbeing, social interaction, work-life balance, technology

Paraphrasing the question

Your introduction requires you to paraphrase the question. Do this by understanding the overall meaning first, then re-expressing it — not by substituting individual words with synonyms. The two most common problems are unnatural language (from translating word-by-word) and misrepresenting the issue.

Common Pitfall

Do not write your introduction until you have fully planned your argument. Your themes and position may shift during planning, and an introduction written too early will no longer represent your essay accurately.

03. Step 2 — Plan

This is where the real thinking happens. A plan that just says "BP1: advantages / BP2: disadvantages" is a description of a structure, not a plan. A useful plan shows your actual ideas, how they connect, and what conclusions they support.

How to build your plan

  1. Use your themes as headings — list them across your planner (e.g. productivity, wellbeing, social interaction)
  2. Brainstorm under each heading — use personal knowledge and experience; ask yourself follow-up questions to develop ideas
  3. Think critically — connect ideas, delete irrelevant ones, and group related ones under an umbrella term
  4. Reach conclusions — your main points should emerge from the evidence you gathered, not be decided in advance
  5. Draft first and last sentences for each paragraph — this frames your argument before you start writing
Umbrella Terms

An umbrella term groups similar ideas under one concept. For example, "need to pay for equipment" and "higher electricity bills" both fall under financial costs. This keeps your paragraphs tightly focused on one central topic and avoids a scattered argument.

Worked Example — Plan Sketch

Question: "To what extent do you agree that working from home is more beneficial?"

Advantages (agree side):

  • No commute → saves time and money → productivity gains
  • Flexible hours → better work-life balance → lower stress
  • Umbrella: personal efficiency and wellbeing

Disadvantages (counterargument):

  • Loss of face-to-face collaboration → weaker team cohesion
  • Blurred home/work boundary → risk of overwork → BUT: manageable with discipline
  • Umbrella: social and professional drawbacks

Once your plan is complete, check: does the evidence support your initial position, or do you need to adjust it? Review your plan for balance and logical flow before you begin writing.

04. Essay Structure

A standard Task 2 essay has four paragraphs. Each element does a specific job — understand the function before writing the sentence.

Introduction

The introduction needs to answer two questions for the reader: what is the general topic and context, and what is the specific issue being discussed? Keep it concise — this is not the place for your argument.

Introduction Skeleton

[Paraphrase of the general topic / context sentence]. [Paraphrase of the specific issue — present the two sides or the question]. [Optional: one-sentence thesis stating your overall position].

Model Introduction

Remote working has become increasingly common in recent decades, and opinions on its merits vary considerably. While some argue that employees are more productive and content outside the office, others believe the professional and social benefits of office life are irreplaceable. This essay will argue that, on balance, working from home offers significant advantages for most workers.

On Thesis Statements

A thesis statement is optional. Include one only if you are certain of your position after planning, and keep it to one brief, accurate sentence. A thesis that misrepresents your actual argument will lower your score, not raise it. Avoid "scope sentences" such as I will discuss both views and give my opinion — these add no value.

Body Paragraphs

Each body paragraph develops one central idea using the PEEL structure. The first and last sentences frame the paragraph — think of them as the P and L — and everything between them must be relevant to the central idea introduced in the opening sentence.

Body Paragraph Skeleton

[Point: one clear central topic sentence]. [Explain: give the reason or mechanism behind the point]. [Expand: show the effect, give an example, or develop the idea further]. [Link: conclude the paragraph and show how it answers the question].

Model Body Paragraph

P — Working from home can offer considerable gains in personal productivity and wellbeing. E — Without a daily commute, employees reclaim several hours each week, time that may be redirected towards focused work or rest. E — In addition, the ability to structure one's own day tends to reduce stress and improve work-life balance, which in turn supports sustained performance. L — For these reasons, remote work may benefit employees more than the traditional office environment.

Conclusion

Write at least two sentences. The first summarises the main ideas in your argument — use the first and last sentences of each body paragraph to help. The second answers the question directly: to what extent you agree, whether one side outweighs the other, or whether the development is positive or negative.

Common Pitfall
  • Writing only one sentence — this is the most common conclusion problem.
  • Summarising only one of your body paragraphs.
  • Introducing a new idea not discussed in the essay.
  • Failing to answer the original question.

Useful conclusion starters

In conclusion, To sum up, Therefore, In my view, On balance, I would argue that

05. PEEL at a Glance

Every sentence in a body paragraph should have a clear purpose. The PEEL structure keeps you accountable.

P
Point Introduce the one central topic of the paragraph. The reader should know exactly what this paragraph is about.
E
Explain Give the reason, cause, or mechanism that supports your point. Why is it true?
E
Expand Develop the idea — show an effect, give a relevant example, or extend the explanation. Avoid inventing statistics.
L
Link Conclude the paragraph and show how the central idea answers or relates to the question. This sentence and the Point sentence should act as a frame — they should be closely connected.
Quick Check

Read only the first and last sentences of each body paragraph. They should form a coherent pair that maps out the paragraph's argument. If they could be reversed without losing meaning, they are well-matched. If they feel disconnected, your central topic may be unclear.

06. Step 3 — Write

Your completed plan is essentially a rough first draft. Follow it as closely as possible, and adjust only when a clearer way to express an idea occurs to you naturally.

Writing strategies

  • Write as if explaining your argument to a knowledgeable colleague — clear and natural, not impressive.
  • Aim for 250–330 words. Below 250 is penalised; above 330 risks wordiness and poor time management.
  • Vary sentence length. Short sentences land hard. Longer ones build and develop. Uniform medium-length sentences flatten everything.
  • Use hedging language — may, could, might, tends to — to signal your stance accurately without overclaiming.
  • Do not write the introduction first. Write it only after planning, when you know what your argument actually contains.
Common Pitfall

Do not attempt to "boost" your language by substituting simple, correct words with complex, unnatural ones. Precision matters more than range. A Band 7 essay often reads simpler than a Band 6 essay — and that is precisely why it scores higher.

07. Step 4 — Check

With a solid plan behind you, checking is faster because you are not trying to fix structural problems — only language ones. Work through three passes:

Pass What to look for Time
Argument Re-read the question, then your essay. Is your position clear throughout? Does every paragraph have one central topic? Are any sentences unclear? (Shorter is usually clearer.) ~5 min
Language Remove or replace repeated words. Use a synonym or pronoun to refer back. Check that hedging language is consistent with your actual position. ~3 min
Proofread Subject-verb agreement; singular/plural nouns; missing prepositions; common spelling errors. Read with your inner voice "louder." ~2 min
Key Idea

If your essays are consistently too short and you struggle to add content during checking, this is a signal to invest more in planning — not more in writing. A well-planned argument is almost always a full-length one.

08. Common Pitfalls Summary

Stage Pitfall Fix
Think Skim-reading the question and misrepresenting the issue Identify all four elements (topic, focus, issue, themes) before writing anything
Think Writing a general, rehearsed opening ("This is a controversial topic") Paraphrase the specific issue in the question — not the broad topic
Plan Planning only structure, not content ("BP1: advantages") Write your actual ideas, connect them, and identify umbrella terms and conclusions
Write Starting with the introduction before knowing the argument Write the introduction last, or at minimum after the plan is complete
Write Long, overloaded sentences to demonstrate grammar One idea per sentence; vary length for rhythm, not to impress
Write Thesis statement that misrepresents the body argument Write the thesis only after planning, and check it against your body paragraphs
Write One-sentence conclusion that does not answer the question Write at least two sentences: summarise the main ideas, then answer the question directly
Check Adding more vocabulary or ideas during checking Only fix what is already there — do not introduce new content at this stage
