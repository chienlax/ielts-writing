IELTS Task 1: Describing Changes

Trend and velocity vocabulary across all five chart types

0. Framework Foundations

Every description of change carries two pieces of information at once. The first is direction: whether a value rises, falls, holds steady, or moves erratically. The second is velocity: how large the movement is, and how quickly it happens. Almost every adverb and verb in this lesson slots into one of those two roles, and the two roles are independent. A sharp fall and a gentle rise share the same grammatical scaffolding.

0.1 The direction-by-velocity matrix

Pick a direction (column) and a velocity (row). The cell gives you a phrase that fits.

Intensity Down Flat Up
Very small edged down, dipped slightly, fell marginally remained stable, held steady edged up, rose slightly, crept up
Moderate fell gradually, declined steadily levelled off, plateaued rose gradually, climbed steadily
Large fell significantly, dropped considerably n/a rose significantly, increased markedly
Extreme plummeted, plunged, fell sharply n/a surged, soared, rose sharply, rocketed

0.2 Verb form versus noun form

Switching between these two forms is one of the easiest ways to add lexical variety. Same meaning, different grammar.

Verb form (subject + verb + adverb) Noun form (there was / saw + adjective + noun)
Sales rose sharply.There was a sharp rise in sales.
The figure fell gradually.The figure saw a gradual fall.
Demand increased significantly.Demand experienced a significant increase.
Prices fluctuated.Prices showed fluctuations.

0.3 The velocity scale

The same idea visualised as a slope. As you move right, the movement intensifies.

The velocity scale, low intensity on the left, accelerating on the right.

0.4 Off-scale vocabulary: stability and fluctuation

Two situations sit outside the velocity scale because they describe a lack of clean directional movement.

  • Stable: remained stable, held steady, plateaued, levelled off, stayed constant
  • Fluctuating: fluctuated, wavered, showed slight fluctuations
Watch out

'Oscillated' is only correct for a needle on a dial, a pendulum, or a wave. It does not mean 'went up and down a bit'. Use fluctuated instead.

'Exponentially' requires the rate of increase to accelerate each period. If the line is straight and steep, the word you want is sharply, not exponentially.

0.5 The "relative" modifiers

These adjust a value against context, not against an absolute size. Useful when a figure looks small in isolation but is significant against the others, or vice versa.

  • relatively small / large / stable
  • only 6 million (small in this dataset)
  • just over 50% / just under 200 grams

1. Line Graph

Line graphs are the natural home of trend vocabulary. Every adverb on the velocity scale applies, and chained verb phrases ("rose sharply before levelling off") are expected.

1.1 What it contributes

Continuous change over time. The reader sees the shape of the movement directly, so the writing must match what the eye sees. A wobble described as a "dramatic decline" loses marks for accuracy.

1.2 Annotated graph

Six segments, six different phrases. Each describes both direction and velocity.

1.3 Example sentences

Model sentences from the graph above

Between 2000 and 2002, the figure rose sharply, climbing from roughly 10 to 75. It then plateaued for two years before dipping slightly in 2006.

From 2006 onwards, the line climbed steadily, before surging to a peak of just under 100 in 2010. This level was short-lived, and the figure plummeted over the final period, falling back to around 50.

1.4 Useful sentence patterns

  • Chaining: X rose sharply before levelling off at Y.
  • Despite + noun: Despite a slight dip in 2006, the overall trend was upward.
  • After + ing: After climbing steadily for four years, the figure peaked at Y.
  • Reaching a peak/low: The figure reached a peak of Y in 2010, then fell back to Z.
Traps
  • "A rising trend" describes the overall direction. The line is allowed to wobble within it. Do not call the trend "inconsistent" because of one dip.
  • Do not pair sharply with a small move. "Fell sharply from 50% to 49%" misrepresents the data. Use edged down or fell marginally.
  • Crossover language: "X overtook Y" means one passed the other. "X and Y converged" means they met without crossing. They are not interchangeable.
  • Always anchor your phrase to a time reference (in 2006, between 2002 and 2004, over the final two years). Adverbs alone are weak.

2. Bar Chart

The bar chart splits into two sub-cases, and the vocabulary shifts between them. Identifying which sub-case you have is the first thing to do.

2.1 Sub-case A: bars over time

If the x-axis is time (years, decades, months), treat the chart exactly like a line graph. Every adverb on the velocity scale applies. The bars are just a different visual for the same underlying movement.

Bars over time, line-graph vocabulary applies directly.

Useful phrasing

Bar chart over time

The figure climbed steadily from 2010 to 2014. After dipping slightly in 2016, it rose more sharply over the final four years, reaching a peak of 175 in 2020.

2.2 Sub-case B: snapshot comparison

If the x-axis lists categories (countries, age groups, product types) and there is no time element, no change is happening. Trend vocabulary does not apply. The toolkit here is comparison.

Snapshot bar chart, no time element. Use comparison vocabulary only.

Comparison vocabulary

FunctionPhrases
Much higherconsiderably higher than, far above, significantly more than, nearly double
Slightly highermarginally higher than, slightly above, just over
Roughly the sameroughly equal to, on a par with, at a similar level to, comparable to
Slightly lowermarginally lower than, just below
Much lowerconsiderably lower than, far below, a fraction of, around half
Rankingthe highest, the second-highest, the next-highest, by far the lowest
Snapshot bar chart

The UK had the highest figure at 170, considerably more than any other country. The USA and France were roughly equal, at around 130 and 125 respectively, while Germany's figure was around half that of the UK. Japan was by far the lowest, at just 35.

The biggest trap in the whole topic

Writing "the figure for men rose to 60% while women fell to 40%" on a snapshot bar chart. Nothing rose or fell. Those are simply the two values being compared. Use comparison language instead: "Men accounted for 60%, considerably more than women at 40%."

This mistake is common because students reach for trend vocabulary by reflex. Always check the x-axis first.

3. Pie Chart

Pie charts work in proportions, not absolute changes. With one pie, there is no change to describe at all. With two or more pies, change vocabulary returns, but applied to shares of a whole rather than raw figures.

3.1 Single pie: proportion vocabulary only

The relevant toolkit is fractions and shares. No trend adverbs.

FunctionPhrases
Verbs of proportionaccounted for, made up, represented, constituted, comprised
Large sharesthe majority, over half, nearly two-thirds, the largest share
Medium sharesa quarter, a third, just under half, around 40%
Small sharesa small proportion, a marginal share, the smallest segment, just under 10%

3.2 Two pies: change applied to proportions

This is the more common exam case. Two pies show the same categories at two points in time, or for two different groups. Now trend verbs return, but the subject of the verb is "the share of X" or "the proportion of Y", not the raw figure.

Two pies with the changes between them annotated.

Useful patterns

  • The share of B doubled, rising from 30% to 60%.
  • A's proportion fell by half, dropping from 50% to 25%.
  • The slice for C contracted slightly, from 20% to 15%.
  • B overtook A as the largest share.
Two-pie comparison

Between 2000 and 2020, the composition of the three categories changed considerably. The share of A fell by half, dropping from 50% to 25%, while the proportion for B doubled, rising from 30% to 60% to become the largest category. C's slice contracted slightly, from 20% to 15%.

Traps
  • Percentage vs percentage points. If a share moves from 20% to 30%, that is a 10 percentage-point rise, but a 50% relative rise. Both are correct in different ways. Pick one framing and stay consistent. The safest move is to say "rose by 10 percentage points, from 20% to 30%".
  • The total is always 100%. If one slice grows, another must shrink. Making this trade-off visible in the writing is often the natural overview sentence.
  • Do not say "X is bigger" without saying "X's share is bigger". The raw value of A might still be larger in absolute terms; only the proportion is shown. Stay precise.

4. Table

A table holds no inherent visual structure, so the vocabulary depends entirely on what the table is actually showing. The first job is to identify the table's shape, then borrow the toolkit from the appropriate earlier section. There is no "table vocabulary" of its own.

4.1 The diagnostic step: what kind of table is this?

Before reaching for any phrase, identify which of these three structures the table has. Each one points to a different vocabulary set.

Table type Tell-tale signs Vocabulary to use
Time series Years, decades, or dates across the top or down the side Line-graph toolkit (Section 1): rose, fell, climbed steadily, plateaued, etc.
Snapshot comparison Categories (countries, age groups, products) with one set of values, no time element Snapshot bar-chart toolkit (Section 2.2): considerably more than, roughly equal to, by far the lowest
Proportional Figures expressed as percentages that sum to 100% Pie-chart toolkit (Section 3): accounted for, made up, the majority of, a marginal share

4.2 Worked example: a single table, three lenses

The same table can be read multiple ways, and the vocabulary changes with the reading. Below is a small table with three different "lenses" applied.

Country 2000 2010 2020
UK405572
USA626058
Japan355054
India122865
Figure X across four countries, 2000 to 2020.

Lens 1: time series (read across rows)

Trend vocabulary applies

The UK figure climbed steadily over the two decades, rising from 40 to 72. India's figure rose most dramatically, increasing more than fivefold from just 12 in 2000 to 65 in 2020. The USA, in contrast, declined gradually from 62 to 58.

Lens 2: snapshot comparison (read down a single column)

Comparison vocabulary applies

In 2000, the USA's figure was the highest at 62, considerably above the UK and Japan, both at around 35 to 40. India was by far the lowest at just 12. By 2020, the picture had shifted: the UK led at 72, narrowly ahead of India at 65, while the USA had fallen to roughly the same level as Japan.

Lens 3: ranking shifts (track each country's position over time)

Trend + ranking vocabulary

India overtook both the USA and Japan during this period to become the second-highest by 2020. The UK maintained its upward trajectory throughout, while the USA's ranking fell from first to last among the four.

4.3 Organising a table answer

The hardest part of a table is not vocabulary, it is what to leave out. A table can have twenty cells, and listing all of them produces an answer that reads like a database dump. Two strategies work.

  • Group by similarity: bundle countries or categories that follow the same trend together, then contrast with the ones that don't. ("The UK and Japan both climbed steadily, while the USA declined and India surged.")
  • Group by ranking: describe the top two or three, then the bottom one or two. ("The two highest figures throughout were X and Y, while Z remained consistently the lowest.")
Traps specific to tables
  • Listing every cell. "In 2010, the figure was 23. In 2011, it was 25. In 2012, it was 26..." This is the most common error. Tables still need trends and comparisons, not enumeration.
  • Mismatched vocabulary. Using "rose sharply" on a snapshot-style table where no time is shown. Always check whether time is on one axis before reaching for trend verbs.
  • Forgetting the overview. A table answer still needs an overview that captures the main pattern. Rank the rows or columns to find it: who started highest, who ended highest, which moved the most.
  • Spurious precision. If figures are 23.7 and 24.1, do not call this a "noticeable rise". Use edged up or remained virtually unchanged. The numbers can lie if the adverb is wrong.
  • Ignoring rate when values cluster. If most countries look similar in raw values, the meaningful comparison is often the rate of change rather than the absolute figure. India in the table above is at 65, lower than the UK's 72, but its growth rate is the dramatic story.

5. Mixed Chart

Mixed charts (a bar chart alongside a line graph, two pies plus a table, and so on) add no new vocabulary. Everything you have learned in Sections 1 to 4 applies. The challenge here is organisation and linking, which is mostly about flagging relationships between the two visuals.

5.1 Two common combinations

  • Two related datasets: for example, a line graph of unemployment and a bar chart of inflation over the same period. The reader expects you to show how the two move together (or in opposition).
  • Two different views of similar information: for example, a pie chart of energy sources in one year and a bar chart of energy consumption over a decade. Here the link is thematic, not mathematical.

5.2 Linking vocabulary

This is the one genuinely new contribution of mixed charts: the words that bridge the two visuals.

RelationshipLinking phrases
Moves in parallelcorrespondingly, in parallel, mirroring this, similarly
Moves in oppositionin contrast, conversely, on the other hand, whereas
Happens alongsidemeanwhile, at the same time, over the same period
Linking two charts

While the bar chart shows unemployment climbing steadily between 2008 and 2012, the line graph reveals that, over the same period, consumer confidence fell sharply. The two trends moved in opposition, suggesting a clear inverse relationship.

Traps
  • Two essays glued together. Writing one paragraph about chart A and one about chart B without ever connecting them loses marks on Task Achievement. The rubric asks for "comparisons where relevant", and the relevance here is between the two visuals.
  • Forcing a connection that does not exist. If the two charts are genuinely unrelated, say so plainly: "the two charts cover different aspects and show no clear correlation". Better than inventing a link.
  • Duplicating data points across paragraphs. Each figure should appear once. If chart B simply restates chart A in a different form, describe the data once and note the format difference in passing.

Final reminders before the exam
  • Always anchor a trend phrase to a time reference. "Rose sharply" alone is weak; "rose sharply between 2010 and 2014" is complete.
  • Match the intensity of your adverb to the actual size of the move. A change from 50 to 49 is not "sharp".
  • Identify your chart type before you reach for vocabulary. Snapshot bar charts and single pies do not take trend verbs.
  • Vary verb and noun forms across the answer for Lexical Resource marks. Do not use "rose" five times when "climbed", "increased", and "a rise" are available.
  • Group your data before writing. Listing every cell is the fastest way to a Band 6.
