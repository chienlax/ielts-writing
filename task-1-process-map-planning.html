Process & Map Tasks

A focused planning guide for the two Task 1 question types that break the chart rulebook. Covers every sub-type in the project and walks through the scratchpad on four worked examples.

01. Why this guide exists

The three-step pre-writing framework holds for every Task 1 question. Notice details, identify key features, group logically, all inside three minutes. What changes between question types is the substance of each step.

For a line graph, Step 2 means listing trends. For a process, Step 2 means grouping steps into stages. For a map, Step 2 means cataloguing what has been added, removed, kept, or replaced. The scratchpad architecture is the same; the columns and the questions are not.

This guide assumes familiarity with the chart-based scratchpad from the main guide. It covers only what changes for process and map tasks.

Key Idea

Process tasks call for stages, the way a chart task calls for trends. Map tasks call for an inventory of changes, the way a chart task calls for data points. Bring chart-based instincts to either and the answer drifts into list-mode, which caps the Task Achievement score at Band 5 or 6.

The shared 3-minute budget

Timing is identical across all task types. Allow 30 seconds for Step 1, around two minutes for Steps 2 and 3 combined, then start writing. The substance changes; the clock does not.

Computer-based scratch surface

Whether the test centre gives you a laminated whiteboard, paper, or an on-screen notepad, the layout in this guide works on all three. Write a one-line strip across the top (Step 1), a compact table below it (Step 2), and a two-line paragraph plan at the bottom (Step 3).

02. Process Tasks

Group steps into stages · passive voice common · avoid first/then/finally chains

A process task shows a sequence: how something is made, how something occurs in nature, or how something works. The task statement usually uses words like diagram, process, stages, life cycle, manufacture, or produce. The scratchpad's job is to convert a busy diagram into a small number of grouped stages that drive the body paragraphs.

Sub-types you will encounter

The project contains eleven process tasks (img 0060 through 0070). They fall into six recognisable patterns, and the scratchpad adapts slightly to each.

Type 1 Linear manufacturing Single chain from raw material to product, possibly with one branch. Sugar (0068) · Instant noodles (0067) · Smoked fish (0061) · Canned fruits (0062)
Type 2 Branching outputs One process splits into two or more final products. Cocoa beans (0066) · Pulp & paper (0060)
Type 3 Natural cycle A continuous loop with no fixed start or end. Carbon cycle (0069)
Type 4 Natural progression A natural phenomenon shown in successive stages. Sand dune formation (0064)
Type 5 Parallel comparison Two or more separate processes compared side by side. Recruitment, three companies (0065)
Type 6 Structural-functional A static device shown with arrows indicating how it works. Water filter (0063) · Glass recycling (0070)
Identify the sub-type first

The sub-type determines how you group stages in Step 3. A linear process gives two stages of unequal size. A branching process needs the branch point named as a separator. A parallel comparison needs one paragraph per process, with comparisons made explicit. Take 5 seconds to classify before starting the scratchpad.

1
Noticing details - the process details strip ~30 seconds

Read the diagram's metadata before tracing any arrow. The strip captures the introduction sentence and the overview-level facts in one line.

What to capture

  • Process type: mechanical, manual, natural, or mixed. This shapes the overview.
  • Number of steps: count the labelled boxes or icons.
  • Where it happens: factory, field, natural environment, or multiple locations.
  • Raw material to final product: trace what enters at the start and what comes out at the end. Note any intermediate forms (plant, juice, syrup, sugar).
  • Time markers: rare, but a number like "12 to 18 months" is a key feature.
  • Branches or merges: circle the arrow split or the inputs that combine.
  • Label conventions: most labels use the -ing form (growing, harvesting). A noun label (centrifuge, evaporator) names a device; it has no verb form.
Computer-based adaptation - details strip
Write one compact line across the top of your scratch surface:

Type | N steps | Where | Raw → Final | Time | Branches?

Everything needed for the introduction and the overview is now off-screen and in your notes.
2
Identifying key features - the stage table ~90 seconds

This is where the rookie error appears most often: treating every step as a key feature, then producing a flat list. A step is one part of the process. A stage is broader and groups several steps. The body paragraphs follow stages.

How to group steps into stages

Three signals tend to mark a stage boundary:

  • Change in form. When the material's physical state changes (solid to liquid, raw to processed, plant to juice), a new stage typically begins.
  • Change in location. From field to factory, from sea to port, from outdoor to packaging line.
  • Change in agent. A shift from manual to mechanical, or from human-driven to automated.
  • Explicit labelling. If the diagram already names "Stage 1, Stage 2, Stage 3" (as in cocoa beans 0066 or glass recycling 0070), the work is done for you.

The umbrella term column

Each stage needs a single noun phrase that covers all its steps. The overview should use these umbrella terms rather than the specific step labels. Common umbrella terms include farming, harvesting, processing, refining, packaging, distribution. The skill is choosing one that fits without sounding generic.

Key Idea

Treating the first and last steps as a "trend" produces the most common Band 5 process overview: "The process starts with farming and ends with dry sugar." That sentence skips everything in between. The overview must summarise the stages rather than the endpoints.

Flags to mark

SymbolUse
★Form-change point (plant becomes liquid, juice becomes syrup)
⤶Branch or merge point
!Unusual feature (two methods shown for one step, time given for one step only)
≅Similar to a previous step (worth a comparison)
Computer-based adaptation - stage table

Write a four-column table: step number, label, what happens (3-5 words), stage. Fill it in by following the arrows on screen, jotting on your scratchpad, and looking back. The table need not be neat; rough labels and flags are enough.

3
Grouping - the paragraph plan ~30 seconds

The default is one paragraph per stage. For a process with two clear stages, this gives two body paragraphs of roughly equal length. For three stages, decide whether to give each its own paragraph or to combine two shorter ones.

Plan format

Below the stage table, write two or three short lines:

  • P1: [stage name], steps [1, 2, ...]
  • P2: [stage name], steps [N, N+1, ...]
  • Compare: [any comparisons available]

What to avoid

  • Sentence-opener templates. Starting every sentence with "First, ... Then, ... Next, ... Finally, ..." reads as mechanical and signals Band 6. Vary openers using time clauses (Once the crop has been harvested) and reduced relative clauses (The juice is passed through a filter, purifying it).
  • Listing every step in the overview. The overview names the stages, never the steps.
  • Copying diagram labels verbatim. The diagram says "Growing"; the answer says "The sugar cane is grown" or "The crop takes 12 to 18 months to grow."
Verb pattern check

Process tasks lean heavily on the passive (The juice is extracted, The plants are crushed). Note in your scratchpad which verbs work passively. Intransitive verbs (fall, appear, become, arrive) cannot be made passive and need a transitive alternative (drop, emerge).

Worked example 1: Sugar from sugar cane (img 0068)

Task

The diagram below shows the manufacturing process for making sugar from sugar cane. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

What the diagram shows

Seven numbered steps: growing (12 to 18 months), harvesting (two methods shown, by machine or by hand), crushing, purifying juice through a limestone filter, an evaporator that turns juice into syrup, a centrifuge that separates sugar crystals from syrup, and finally drying and cooling.

Why this plan works

The two stages are uneven (2 steps vs 5 steps), and that is fine. P1 stays short because farming is short; P2 carries the bulk of the description because processing is where the form-changes happen. The body paragraphs mirror the diagram's natural rhythm rather than fighting it.

Worked example 2: Cocoa beans, branching variant (img 0066)

Task

The diagram shows the stages of processing cocoa beans. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

What the diagram shows

The diagram already names three stages explicitly. Stage 1 covers picking, removing shells, and drying whole beans. After pressing, the process branches: one path goes to further pressing, producing cocoa cake which is then ground into cocoa powder or cocoa liquor for industry. The other path adds sugar in further refining to produce industrial chocolate, which becomes finished chocolate products for consumers. The branch is the structural feature of the task.

Step 2 - Stage table
StageStepsOutputsNotes
1Picking, shelling, dryingdried whole beansLinear; shells removed as by-product

P2 - Stages 2-3 (the branch) Pressing splits into two paths. Path A (further pressing): cocoa cake, then ground into powder/liquor. Path B (further refining + sugar): industrial chocolate, then finished products. End users differ.
Comparisons: the two branch outputs (powder/liquor for industry vs chocolate for consumers); sugar only enters path B.
Overview captures: type (mechanical), 3 labelled stages, branching at pressing, two distinct end-products.
Branch handling

Where a process branches, name the branch point in the overview and devote space to comparing the two paths. The branch is what makes the task interesting, and the assessor expects to see the comparison made explicit. Phrases that work: at this point the process diverges; one path leads to ..., the other to ...; whereas path A produces X, path B yields Y.

03. Map & Plan Tasks

Catalogue changes · passive past or future tense · before/after paragraphs

A map or plan task shows two or more views of the same place, normally before-and-after or present-and-future. The diagrams might depict an island, a building floor plan, a town across decades, or an airport pre- and post-redevelopment. The scratchpad's job is to convert a busy comparison into a small inventory of changes, grouped by domain.

Sub-types you will encounter

The project contains eleven map tasks (img 0071 through 0081). They cluster into five patterns based on time relationship and spatial scope.

Type 1 Past & present Two maps, with the second being "now". Simple past for the first, present perfect or simple present for the second. Sports court (0071) · Museum (0074) · Tourist destination (0075) · College campus (0077) · Library (0078)
Type 2 Present & future Two maps, with the second being the planned redevelopment. Future tense throughout the second paragraph (will be, is going to be). Conference centre (0081) · Airport (0076) · Community centre (0073, framed as past/present but tense logic is the same)
Type 3 Multi-period Three or more time points showing gradual development. Calls for narrative across paragraphs, with the largest changes flagged. Willington (0079) · Meadowside/Fonton (0072)
Type 4 Single planning map Only one map, showing a planned development. No before-state. Describe the proposed layout grouped by zone. Factory complex & housing (0080)
Type 5 Internal floor plan Same physical area, but the focus is fixtures and fittings inside a building. Vocabulary leans on furniture and facilities terms. Library (0078) · Community centre (0073) · Conference centre (0081)
Identify the tense logic first

Before noting a single change, look at the time labels. Past+present needs the simple past and present perfect. Present+future needs future tense throughout the second paragraph. Multi-period needs careful sequencing. Getting tense wrong is the single most common Band 5/6 mistake in this task type.

1
Noticing details - the map details strip ~30 seconds

The metadata for a map differs from a chart. There are no axes, no units, no categories in the chart sense. What replaces them is the spatial frame: dates, scale, compass, and the key.

What to capture

  • Place type: island, town, building floor, campus, airport. Determines vocabulary domain.
  • Time labels: the dates or framing of each map ("1990 / Now", "Present / Future", "1962 / 1985 / 2000").
  • Key: the box explaining symbols (footpaths, vehicle tracks, building types). Always check.
  • Scale: if shown, note it for use when describing distances or sizes.
  • Compass: if shown, available for large-area orientation. Use sparingly; do not append a compass point to every detail.
  • Boundaries: natural features (river, sea, woods) and infrastructure (road, railway) often anchor the map.
Computer-based adaptation - details strip
Write one compact line across the top of your scratch surface:

Place | Times | Key? | Scale? | Compass? | Boundaries

The strip tells you which tense pair to use and which vocabulary set the answer will draw on.
Common slip

Skipping the key wastes information the examiner expects you to use. If the key explains that a dotted line means a footpath, the answer should distinguish footpaths from roads. Symbols carry information you might otherwise miss.

2
Identifying key features - the change inventory ~90 seconds

The chart inventory asks: what went up, what went down? The map inventory asks: what was added, removed, kept, expanded, reduced, replaced, or repositioned? Each change type implies a specific verb form and a specific tense.

The seven change types

Change typeSymbolTypical verb pattern
Added+ NEW"X has been built / added / constructed" or "X will be added"
Removed− GONE"X has been removed / demolished" or "X will be removed"
Expanded / enlarged↑ BIG"X has been extended / widened / lengthened"
Reduced / shrunk↓ SMALL"X has been reduced / narrowed / shortened"
Replaced↻ REPL"X has been replaced by Y" or "Y has been built where X used to be"
Repositioned⇄ MOVE"X has been moved / relocated"
Retained= KEEP"X remains / has been retained / is still in place"

The umbrella term column

Each change belongs to a vocabulary domain. The standard umbrella terms for map tasks fall into a small number of categories:

  • Accommodation: housing, blocks of flats, huts, dormitories
  • Amenities: reception, restaurants, cafes, shops, toilets
  • Leisure facilities: pools, gyms, sports courts, dance studios
  • Access / transport: roads, footpaths, vehicle tracks, piers, car parks, stations
  • Natural features: trees, vegetation, beach, river, gardens, parks
  • Infrastructure: bridges, power lines, sewerage
  • Internal layout (for floor plans): meeting rooms, halls, offices, storage, kitchens
Key Idea

The umbrella term is what the overview uses. Writing "several new sports facilities have been added" is overview-level. Writing "a leisure pool, two dance studios, and a gym have been added" is body-paragraph level. Choose the umbrella term in Step 2 so that the overview almost writes itself in Step 3.

Computer-based adaptation - change inventory

Write a four-column table: feature, Time 1 state, Time 2 state, change type. Add a fifth column for the umbrella group if there is room. This is the densest part of the scratchpad and the one to spend the most time on.

3
Grouping - the paragraph plan ~30 seconds

The default is two paragraphs, organised by time. The first paragraph describes the before-state (shorter, since the focus is on what changed). The second describes the after-state, with the changes themselves highlighted (longer, since this is where the task is scored).

Paragraph plan formats

Three groupings tend to work well, depending on the task:

  1. Before / after. The classic, and the one to default to. Works for past-present and present-future maps.
  2. Indoor / outdoor. Useful when the changes split naturally along that boundary (sports centre with internal and external facilities, museum with interior arrangement and surrounding grounds).
  3. Retained / changed. Useful when several features stay the same and the rest are transformed. P1 covers what is unchanged, P2 covers what has been altered.

What to avoid

  • Compass-based grouping. Dividing the map into north, south, east, and west and writing one paragraph on each produces a flat list. The reader cannot tell what changed.
  • Describing each map separately. "The first map shows... The second map shows..." reads as two disconnected descriptions and undermines the comparison task.
  • Naming every location precisely. "X is to the left of Y, which is on the east side of Z" is the symptom. Mention location when it matters (a feature has moved, the building has expanded in a specific direction), and otherwise leave it.
  • Personal opinion or evaluation. Adjectives like beautiful, impressive, modern, well-developed introduce subjectivity and reduce the Task Achievement score.
The tense question

In map tasks, the wrong tense in the wrong paragraph is a Grammatical Range and Accuracy red flag. Ask the four questions in Step 2: What did it look like before? What has changed? What has been added? What has stayed the same? The variety in the questions produces variety in the verbs.

Worked example 1: University sports court, 1990 vs Now (img 0071)

Task

The two maps below give information about the facilities of a university sports court in 1990 and the present. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

What the maps show

In 1990, the site is largely outdoor: an outdoor pool, a separate small cafe, a small changing room, a park area with many trees, and a small car park. By the present, the layout has changed dramatically. A single indoor building now contains a fitness centre, indoor pool, changing room, cafe, and reception. Outside the building, there are tennis courts and a basketball court. The park and the car park are gone.

The overview names the dominant transformation (outdoor to indoor) without listing facilities. The change inventory shows that one feature ★ the pool, drives the whole story: it moved indoors, and a building grew around it. The body paragraphs trace this through the umbrella terms (leisure, amenities, natural, access), avoiding a compass-based list.

Future-tense discipline

Map tasks with a planned future invite the future passive throughout the second paragraph: will be added, will be extended, is going to be relocated. Beware of "is planned to be"; the correct construction is "is planned" or simply "will be ...". Do not write "plan to remove" as a passive (cannot use the verb plan that way).

05. Common Pitfalls at the Planning Stage

Pitfall 1
Process: treating steps as trends. "The process begins with sugar cane and ends with sugar." This summarises only the endpoints and skips the stages between them. The overview must name the stages between the endpoints.
Pitfall 2
Process: copying diagram labels. The diagram says "Growing"; the answer should say "The sugar cane is grown." Verbatim copying from the question paper is penalised.
Pitfall 3
Process: First / Then / Next / Finally chain. Repetitive cohesive devices flag Band 6. Mix in time clauses (Once the juice has been purified) and reduced relatives (The plants are crushed, extracting the juice).
Pitfall 4
Map: describing each diagram separately. "The first map shows... The second map shows..." reads as two disconnected paragraphs and undermines the comparison task. Structure around what changed, not around which diagram you are looking at.
Pitfall 5
Map: only simple present tense. Using only "there is / there are" across both paragraphs signals you are describing pictures rather than summarising change. Force tense variety with the four questions: what did it look like, what has changed, what has been added, what has been removed.
Pitfall 6
Map: compass-point lists. Naming the precise compass direction for every feature ("X is to the east of Y, which is to the south of Z") buries the changes under location data. Use compass points only when they convey a key feature (a building extended in a specific direction).
Pitfall 7
Map: evaluative language. Adjectives like beautiful, impressive, modern, well-developed introduce personal opinion and reduce the Task Achievement score. The task is to summarise facts, not to assess them.
Pitfall 8
Map: wrong passive construction. "The outdoor courts are planned to be removed" is incorrect. Use "will be removed" or "are to be removed". The verb plan does not work passively in this construction.
Pitfall 9
Both: skipping Step 1 entirely. Starting Step 2 without the strip means working from screen-glances, which costs more time than the strip would have taken. Thirty seconds spent on the strip saves several minutes during writing.

Scope of this guide: This document covers only the planning phase (Steps 1, 2, and 3 of the pre-writing process) for process and map tasks. The writing phase and post-writing checks are covered in the main IELTS Task 1 Complete Guide.

Time budget recap: Three minutes total for planning. Spend it where the substance lives: ~30 seconds on the details strip, ~90 seconds on the stage table or change inventory, and ~30 seconds on the paragraph plan.
